01 / About the Topic

Why this matters right now

In today's digital world, information spreads faster than ever, which makes it increasingly important to tell apart misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation — three terms that get used interchangeably but describe very different problems.

Unlike traditional media, where content passes through an editor before publication, anyone can publish instantly online — and algorithms tend to reward whatever is sensational or emotionally charged, letting false claims outrun factual corrections. Advances in AI have made fabricated images, video, and audio harder to spot than ever, which is exactly why source verification and critical thinking matter more now than at any point before.

The stakes are concrete: disinformation can sway elections and erode trust in institutions; health misinformation pushes people toward harmful choices; malinformation — true information weaponized — fuels doxxing and reputational harm. Knowing which one you're looking at determines the right response.

02 / Main Content

Three words, three different problems

Truthfulness and intent are the two dials that separate these terms. Tap around the site to see how they diverge.

False · No intent to deceive

Misinformation

False or inaccurate information shared without the intention to deceive — usually from misunderstanding, poor fact-checking, or genuine belief that it's true.

FALSE + no intent to harm
False · Deliberate

Disinformation

Deliberately false information created or spread to mislead people for political, financial, or social gain.

FALSE + intent to deceive
True · Weaponized

Malinformation

Genuinely true information shared intentionally to cause harm — leaking private documents, exposing personal data, or revealing confidential records.

TRUE + intent to harm
